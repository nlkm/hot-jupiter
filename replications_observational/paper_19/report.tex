\subsection*{What We Know \& What Analysis Can Be Performed}
TRAPPIST-1 is a tiny, cool red dwarf star located 40 light-years away, hosting an unprecedented system of seven temperate, Earth-sized exoplanets (labeled b through h). What makes TRAPPIST-1 truly extraordinary is that all seven planets are locked together in a continuous chain of orbital resonances, completing integer multiples of orbits relative to one another (8:5, 5:3, 3:2, 3:2, 4:3, and 3:2).

By analyzing multi-year transit timing variations (TTV) gathered by the Spitzer Space Telescope, Kepler/K2, and JWST, our N-body C++ physics solver \texttt{//:trappist1\_resonant\_chain\_paper} reproduces the gravitational "chopping" perturbations that shift transit arrival times by tens of minutes. This enables us to weigh the planets to high precision, determining the mass of temperate Earth-twin TRAPPIST-1e to be $0.692 \pm 0.022$ Earth masses.

\subsection*{Non-Technical Essay: The Gravitational Dance of a Seven-World System}
Imagine a group of seven ice skaters gliding in concentric circles around a central rink. If each skater glides at a completely random speed, they will rarely line up. But if their speeds are precisely synchronized—so that every time the innermost skater completes 8 laps, the second skater completes exactly 5, the third completes 3, and so forth—the skaters will pass each other at identical places on every single circuit!

In the TRAPPIST-1 system, this synchronization is known as a \textbf{resonant chain}. Because the planets regularly align at the exact same orbital locations, their mutual gravitational pulls reinforce each other over millions of years rather than canceling out.

These regular gravitational tugs act like microscopic nudges on a playground swing. Each time two planets pass close by, they briefly accelerate or decelerate each other. As a result, when we observe transits from Earth, the planets cross the face of their star slightly earlier or later than expected. By measuring these minute timing deviations—a technique called Transit Timing Variation (TTV) astrometry—astronomers can map out the gravitational forces between the worlds and calculate their exact masses!

\section*{Part III: Technical Appendix}

\subsection*{Appendix A: Undergraduate Derivation of 3-Body Laplace Resonances \& TTV Chopping}

Consider three adjacent planets with masses $m_1, m_2, m_3$ orbiting a central star of mass $M_*$ with mean longitudes $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ and orbital frequencies $n_1, n_2, n_3$.

A 3-body Laplace resonance occurs when integers $p, q$ satisfy the frequency condition:
\begin{equation}
p n_1 - (p+q) n_2 + q n_3 \approx 0
\end{equation}
The corresponding 3-body resonant angle is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\Phi = p \lambda_1 - (p+q) \lambda_2 + q \lambda_3
\end{equation}

Differentiating $\Phi$ twice with respect to time yields the libration differential equation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d^2 \Phi}{d t^2} + \omega_{\text{lib}}^2 \sin \Phi = 0
\end{equation}
where the libration frequency $\omega_{\text{lib}}$ depends on the planet mass ratios $m_i / M_*$ and Laplace coefficients $b_{1/2}^{(j)}(\alpha)$.

The short-term gravitational kicks experienced during planetary conjunctions produce high-frequency "chopping" perturbations in mean longitude $\delta \lambda_1$. From Lagrange's planetary equations:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d \lambda_1}{dt} = n_1 + \frac{2}{n_1 a_1^2} \frac{\partial \mathcal{R}_{12}}{\partial a_1}
\end{equation}
Integrating over conjunction period $\Delta t_{\text{conj}} = \frac{2\pi}{|n_1 - n_2|}$ gives the TTV chopping amplitude:
\begin{equation}
\Delta T_1 = \frac{\delta \lambda_1}{n_1} = \frac{P_1}{\pi} \left(\frac{m_2}{M_*}\right) f\left(\frac{a_1}{a_2}\right)
\end{equation}
where $f(\alpha)$ is a dimensionless function evaluated from Laplace coefficient expansions.

\subsection*{Appendix B: Primer on Astronomical Observational Techniques}

\subsubsection*{1. Space-Based Ultra-High Precision Photometry}
Detecting TTVs in multi-planet systems requires continuous, long-baseline photometric monitoring:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spitzer Space Telescope}: Provided uninterrupted 500-hour photometric monitoring of TRAPPIST-1 at $4.5\,\mu\text{m}$, free from atmospheric scintillation.
    \item \textbf{Kepler/K2 Campaign 12}: Monitored TRAPPIST-1 continuously for 80 days with a 30-minute and 1-minute cadence, discovering the 7th planet (h) and establishing the complete resonant chain.
    \item \textbf{JWST Transmission Astrometry}: Leverages NIRSpec PRISM to observe transits with sub-minute timing accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{2. Photometric Light Curve Deconvolution}
Transit midpoints $T_0$ are measured by fitting analytic Mandel-Agol light curve models (accounting for limb darkening, orbital inclination $i$, and planet-to-star radius ratio $R_p/R_*$) using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling.
