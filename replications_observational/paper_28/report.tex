\section*{Level 1: For the Non-Scientist — A World of Liquid Rock and Boiling Skies}

\subsection*{The Big Picture}
Imagine a planet so close to its star that a "year" lasts only $18\,\text{hours}$, and the gravitational pull locks it in place so that one face perpetually stares into the scorching stellar furnace while the other face looks out into eternal night. This is \textbf{55 Cancri e}, a "super-Earth" twice the size of our home planet and eight times as massive, located 41 light-years away.

\subsection*{Rivers of Lava and Glass Skies}
On the day-side of 55 Cancri e, temperatures soar past $2400^\circ\text{C}$ ($2700\,\text{K}$)—hot enough to instantly melt solid granite and vaporize iron into gas. The entire sunlit hemisphere is a vast, churning ocean of liquid rock. 

Meanwhile, high-speed atmospheric winds blow the boiling mineral vapors eastward across the surface. This creates an asymmetric "hotspot" shifted $41^\circ$ away from the direct sub-stellar point. On the dark night-side, temperatures drop sharply to $1100^\circ\text{C}$ ($1380\,\text{K}$), causing vaporized silicates to freeze and rain down from the sky as liquid glass.

\section*{Level 2: For the First-Year Undergrad — Radiative Equilibrium \& Stefan-Boltzmann Phase Curves}

\subsection*{1. Sub-Stellar Equilibrium Temperature}
For a tidally locked planet at semi-major axis $a = 0.0154\,\text{AU}$ orbiting a star of luminosity $L_\star \approx 0.58\,L_\odot$ and radius $R_\star \approx 0.94\,R_\odot$, the incident stellar flux at the sub-stellar point ($\theta = 0$) is:
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{inc}} = \frac{L_\star}{4\pi a^2} = \sigma_{\text{SB}} T_\star^4 \left(\frac{R_\star}{a}\right)^2
\end{equation}
Assuming local radiative equilibrium with zero bond albedo ($A_B \approx 0$) and zero heat redistribution, energy balance requires:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{SB}} T_{\text{sub}}^4 = F_{\text{inc}} \implies T_{\text{sub}} = T_\star \sqrt{\frac{R_\star}{a}} \approx (5196\,\text{K}) \sqrt{\frac{0.94 \times 6.96 \times 10^8\,\text{m}}{0.0154 \times 1.496 \times 10^{11}\,\text{m}}} \approx 2700\,\text{K}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{2. Thermal Phase Curve Modulation}
As the planet orbits its host star, an observer on Earth views different fractions of the day and night hemispheres at orbital phase angle $\phi \in [-\pi, +\pi]$:
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{obs}}(\phi) = \frac{1}{\pi d^2} \int_{\text{visible disk}} I(\theta, \psi) \cos\alpha\,dA
\end{equation}
The observed $4.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ secondary eclipse depth $\delta_{\text{ecl}} \approx 130\,\text{ppm}$ corresponds to the peak day-side thermal flux emitted into the line of sight.

\subsection*{1. Magma Ocean Advection \& Atmospheric Drag}
The eastward phase shift of the thermal maximum $\Delta\phi = 41.0^\circ$ is governed by the competition between advective heat transport by high-altitude winds / surface magma currents and radiative cooling:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla T = -\frac{T - T_{\text{eq}}}{\tau_{\text{rad}}} - \frac{\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{F}_{\text{latent}}}{\rho c_p}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{\text{rad}} \approx P / (g \sigma_{\text{SB}} T^3) \approx 10^4\,\text{s}$ is the radiative cooling timescale. Matching $\Delta\phi \approx 41^\circ$ requires an eastward equatorial jet speed $u_{\text{jet}} \approx 1.2 - 2.0\,\text{km/s}$ or vigorous dynamic magma ocean overturning.

\subsection*{2. Mineral Vapor Atmospheric Composition}
Equilibrium vapor pressure above a molten silicate melt at $T = 2700\,\text{K}$ is dominated by $\mathrm{SiO}$, $\mathrm{O_2}$, $\mathrm{O}$, $\mathrm{Na}$, and $\mathrm{K}$:
\begin{equation}
\log_{10} P_{\text{vap}}(\text{bar}) = A - \frac{B}{T} \implies P_{\text{vap}}(2700\,\text{K}) \approx 1.40\,\text{bar}
\end{equation}
JWST NIRCam and MIRI observations constrain volatile depletion ($[\mathrm{H_2O}] < 10^{-4}$), demonstrating that 55 Cancri e is a secondary, heavy-element dominated mineral vapor atmosphere.
