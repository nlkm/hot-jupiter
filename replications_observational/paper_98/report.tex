\section*{Level 1: For the Non-Scientist — The King of All Volcanoes}

\subsection*{The Big Picture}
Towering 13.2 miles (21.3 km) above the plains of Mars, \textbf{Olympus Mons} is the largest volcano in the entire Solar System—nearly three times taller than Mount Everest and large enough to cover the entire state of Arizona!

\subsection*{A Giant Sunken Roof 50 Miles Wide}
At the very top of Olympus Mons lies not a simple crater, but a colossal, multi-ringed depression called a \textbf{nested caldera complex}, spanning \textbf{50 miles (80 km) wide and 2 miles (3.2 km) deep}. When massive pulses of lava erupted out of subterranean magma reservoirs 10 miles deep, the immense support holding up the summit vanished. Like a hollow roof caving in, the summit collapsed downward in six successive piston-like collapse episodes over hundreds of millions of years, creating the stepped amphitheater we see today!

\section*{Level 2: For the Undergrad — Magma Chamber Deflation \& Piston Caldera Collapse}

\subsection*{1. Magma Chamber Evacuation \& Piston Subsidence}
When an underground magma chamber of radius $R_{\text{ch}} \approx 20 - 40\,\text{km}$ at depth $H \approx 15\,\text{km}$ evacuates a volume $\Delta V_{\text{magma}} \approx 10^3 - 10^4\,\text{km}^3$, the overlying volcanic roof undergoes brittle shear along ring faults:
\begin{equation}
\Delta z_{\text{sub}} \approx \frac{\Delta V_{\text{magma}}}{\pi R_{\text{caldera}}^2} \approx 3.2\,\text{km}
\end{equation}
The complex contains 6 coalesced, overlapping collapse craters formed by successive magma withdrawal episodes.

\subsection*{2. Thick Elastic Lithosphere \& Flexural Rigidity}
Because Mars lacks plate tectonics, the stationary mantle plume supported Olympus Mons over a massive elastic lithosphere ($T_e \approx 70 - 100\,\text{km}$). The flexural rigidity:
\begin{equation}
D = \frac{E T_e^3}{12 (1 - \nu^2)} \approx 2.0 \times 10^{24}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}
\end{equation}
prevented the volcano from sinking into the mantle, enabling its unprecedented $21.3\,\text{km}$ height.
