\section*{Level 1: For the Non-Scientist — The Neptune Monster with a Million-Mile Cloak}

\subsection*{The Big Picture}
Located 33 light-years away in the constellation Leo, \textbf{GJ 436b} is a warm, Neptune-sized ice giant orbiting extremely close to a red dwarf star every 2.6 days. While its physical planet disk is relatively modest, it is shrouded in something truly monstrous.

\subsection*{The Behemoth: A Cloud that Blocks Over Half of a Star's Light}
When viewed in visible light, the planet barely dims its star by \textbf{0.69\%} for just 1 hour. But when the Hubble Space Telescope looked in ultraviolet light, it made an astonishing discovery: GJ 436b is wrapped in a titanic, glowing hydrogen cloud dubbed \textbf{``The Behemoth''}, spanning millions of miles. This colossal cometary cloud blocks an incredible \textbf{56\% of the star's ultraviolet light} and takes nearly a full day (\textbf{22 hours}) to transit across the star!

\section*{Level 2: For the Undergrad — Hydrodynamic Escape \& Exospheric Geometry}

\subsection*{1. Low Surface Gravity \& Enhanced Mass Loss}
Because GJ 436b has a much lower surface gravity ($g_p \approx 11.7\,\text{m/s}^2$) and lower mass ($M_p \approx 22.2\,M_\oplus$) than Jupiter-mass planets, its thermosphere is loosely bound. Stellar XUV irradiation ($F_{\text{XUV}} \approx 6.3 \times 10^4\,\text{erg}/(\text{cm}^2\cdot\text{s})$) drives steady photoevaporation:
\begin{equation}
\dot{M} = \frac{3 \epsilon F_{\text{XUV}} R_p^3}{4 G M_p K_{\text{tide}}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{10}\,\text{g/s} \quad (22{,}000\,\text{metric tons per second})
\end{equation}
Over its multi-billion-year lifespan, GJ 436b has shed $\approx 0.1 - 0.2\,M_\oplus$ of its outer hydrogen envelope.

\subsection*{2. Radiation Pressure \& Cometary Tail Kinematics}
Unlike hot Jupiters where strong stellar radiation blows gas straight back, the M-dwarf host star GJ 436 has weak radiation pressure ($a_{\text{rad}} \approx 0.3 - 0.5\,g_*$). Escaping neutral hydrogen atoms slowly lag behind the planet in Keplerian orbits, spreading into a colossal trailing tail extending over $\Delta L \approx 1.5 \times 10^7\,\text{km}$ ($0.1\,\text{AU}$), creating a $22\,\text{hour}$ transit duration.

\subsection*{1. Weak Radiation Pressure Regime \& 3D Exospheric Structure}
The net force on escaping hydrogen atoms in the rotating frame is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = -\frac{G M_*}{r^2}(1 - \beta_{\text{rad}})\hat{\mathbf{r}} - \frac{G M_p}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_p|^3}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_p) - 2m(\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{v}) - m\mathbf{\Omega}\times(\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r})
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{\text{rad}} = F_{\text{rad}}/F_{\text{grav}} \approx 0.34$, allowing Coriolis forces to shape the cloud into a massive leading coma and extended lagging tail.
