\section*{Level 1: For the Non-Scientist — The Doomed Moon of Mars}

\subsection*{The Big Picture}
Mars has two tiny, potato-shaped moons named Phobos (Fear) and Deimos (Dread). While Earth's Moon is slowly drifting away from us at about $3.8\,\text{centimeters per year}$, Phobos is doing the exact opposite: it is spiraling inward toward Mars at $1.82\,\text{centimeters per year}$.

\subsection*{Why Is Phobos Falling?}
Phobos orbits Mars so fast that it completes an entire orbit in just $7\,\text{hours and } 39\,\text{minutes}$—faster than Mars rotates on its axis ($24.6\,\text{hours}$). 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Because Phobos moves faster than Mars spins, the gravitational "tidal bulge" that Phobos raises on Mars always lags behind the moon.
    \item This lagging tidal bulge pulls backward on Phobos like a cosmic brake, stealing its orbital energy and pulling it closer to Mars every single day.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{The Grand Finale: Mars Will Get Rings!}
In roughly $38.5\,\text{million years}$, Phobos will cross the "Roche limit"—the danger zone where Mars' tidal gravity overpowers the internal strength holding the moon together. Because Phobos is a loose "rubble pile" of rocks, Mars will tear it apart into trillions of pieces, creating a stunning planetary ring system around the Red Planet that will last for tens of millions of years.

\section*{Level 2: For the First-Year Undergrad — Tidal Torque Mechanics \& Roche Limits}

\subsection*{1. Derivation of the Tidal Decay Rate}
The tidal torque $\tau_{\text{tide}}$ exerted by a planet of mass $M_{\text{Mars}}$ and radius $R_{\text{Mars}}$ on a satellite of mass $M_{\text{Ph}}$ at semi-major axis $a$ is:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{tide}} = \frac{dL_{\text{orb}}}{dt} = -\frac{3}{2} k_2 \frac{G M_{\text{Ph}}^2 R_{\text{Mars}}^5}{a^6} \sin(2\delta)
\end{equation}
where $\sin(2\delta) \approx 1/Q_{\text{Mars}}$ for small lag angles.
Using Keplerian orbital angular momentum $L_{\text{orb}} = M_{\text{Ph}} \sqrt{G M_{\text{Mars}} a}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dL_{\text{orb}}}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} M_{\text{Ph}} \sqrt{\frac{G M_{\text{Mars}}}{a}} \frac{da}{dt}
\end{equation}
Equating torques yields the fundamental tidal decay differential equation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{da}{dt} = -3 \frac{k_2}{Q_{\text{Mars}}} \sqrt{\frac{G}{M_{\text{Mars}}}} M_{\text{Ph}} R_{\text{Mars}}^5 a^{-11/2}
\end{equation}
Integrating from initial radius $a_0$ to $a(t)$:
\begin{equation}
a(t) = \left[ a_0^{13/2} - \frac{13}{2} \mathcal{K} t \right]^{2/13}, \quad \mathcal{K} \equiv 3 \frac{k_2}{Q_{\text{Mars}}} \sqrt{\frac{G}{M_{\text{Mars}}}} M_{\text{Ph}} R_{\text{Mars}}^5
\end{equation}

\subsection*{2. The Fluid Roche Disruption Boundary}
Tidal forces exceed self-gravity at the fluid Roche limit:
\begin{equation}
a_{\text{Roche}} \approx 1.442 R_{\text{Mars}} \left(\frac{\rho_{\text{Mars}}}{\rho_{\text{Ph}}}\right)^{1/3} = 1.442 (3390\,\text{km}) \left(\frac{3934\,\text{kg/m}^3}{1876\,\text{kg/m}^3}\right)^{1/3} \approx 8950\,\text{km}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{1. Mars Mantle Viscoelastic Tidal Quality Factor}
Radio tracking of spacecraft range-rate residuals yields the secular acceleration of Phobos $\dot{n} = (1.272 \pm 0.015) \times 10^{-3}\,\text{deg/yr}^2$, corresponding to an orbital decay rate $\dot{a} = -1.82 \pm 0.04\,\text{cm/yr}$. 
Inverting for the Martian mantle tidal response parameter gives:
\begin{equation}
\frac{k_2}{Q_{\text{Mars}}} = (1.63 \pm 0.05) \times 10^{-3} \implies Q_{\text{Mars}} = 86.0 \pm 3.0 \quad (\text{for } k_2 = 0.140)
\end{equation}
This low dissipation quality factor reflects partial melting in the deep Martian mantle near the core-mantle boundary (CMB), consistent with InSight seismic detections.

\subsection*{2. Rubble-Pile Shredding and Ring Optical Depth Evolution}
Upon reaching $a \approx 8950\,\text{km}$ at $t = +38.5\,\text{Myr}$, shear stresses overcome the low tensile strength ($\sigma_{\text{tensile}} < 100\,\text{Pa}$) of Phobos' porous interior ($\phi \approx 30\%$). Collisional spreading of mass $M_{\text{Ph}} = 1.07 \times 10^{16}\,\text{kg}$ forms a dense ring between $1.08$ and $2.0\,R_{\text{Mars}}$ with an initial peak optical depth:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{peak}} \approx \frac{3 M_{\text{ring}}}{4 \pi \rho_{\text{grain}} \bar{r}_{\text{grain}} (R_{\text{out}}^2 - R_{\text{in}}^2)} \approx 0.15 - 0.25
\end{equation}
which will persist for $10 - 40\,\text{Myr}$ before Poynting-Robertson drag and atmospheric ablation deposit the dust onto Mars' equatorial belt.
