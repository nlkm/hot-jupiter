\section*{Level 1: For the Non-Scientist — Six Worlds Packed Inside Mercury's Orbit}

\subsection*{The Big Picture}
In 2011, NASA's Kepler space telescope discovered one of the most astonishing planetary systems in the universe: \textbf{Kepler-11}, a single star hosting \textbf{six low-density planets} orbiting so closely together that the inner five would easily fit inside the orbit of Mercury!

\subsection*{The Flat Cosmic Record}
All six planets orbit in almost the exact same flat plane (mutual inclinations $< 1^\circ$). As they race around their star, their gravitational pulls cause their neighbors to cross the star a few minutes early or late. By measuring these tiny \textbf{Transit Timing Variations (TTVs)}, astronomers were able to weigh all six worlds without ever using ground-based telescopes!

\section*{Level 2: For the First-Year Undergrad — TTV Super-Periods \& Laplace Inversion}

\subsection*{1. Resonant Perturbation Super-Periods}
For two planets near a $j:(j-k)$ mean motion resonance with orbital periods $P_1$ and $P_2$, the synodic interaction cycle generates a long-term TTV \textbf{super-period}:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{super}} = \frac{1}{\left| \frac{j}{P_2} - \frac{j-k}{P_1} \right|}
\end{equation}
For planets Kepler-11c ($P_c = 13.025\,\text{days}$) and Kepler-11d ($P_d = 22.687\,\text{days}$), near the 7:4 resonance, the theoretical super-period is $P_{\text{super}} \approx 415\,\text{days}$, creating large, periodic sinusoidal timing swings.

\subsection*{2. Analytical TTV Mass Inversion}
The amplitude of the TTV oscillation on planet 1 driven by the gravitational mass of outer planet 2 ($M_2$) is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta t_1 \approx \frac{P_1}{2\pi} \frac{M_2}{M_\star} f_1(\alpha, e_1, e_2)
\end{equation}
Measuring a $24.5\,\text{minute}$ TTV peak on planet d directly constrains the mass of neighboring planets to high precision ($M_d = 6.1 \pm 0.5\,M_\oplus$).
