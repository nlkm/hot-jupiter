\section*{Level 1: For the Non-Scientist — The Methane Wonderland of Titan}

\subsection*{The Big Picture}
Saturn's largest moon, \textbf{Titan}, is the only moon in the Solar System wrapped in a thick atmosphere—even denser than Earth's air! If you stood on Titan, you wouldn't need a pressurized spacesuit, just a warm coat and an oxygen mask. 

\subsection*{Rivers, Lakes, and Methane Rain}
Titan is a frozen mirror of Earth. But instead of liquid water, Titan has a full hydrological cycle based on \textbf{liquid methane} ($\mathrm{CH_4}$) and ethane ($\mathrm{C_2H_6}$). Giant polar seas like \textit{Kraken Mare} and \textit{Ligeia Mare} are filled with liquid natural gas hundreds of feet deep, fed by methane rain clouds and river deltas!

\subsection*{The Fast-Spinning Sky}
Titan takes 16 days to complete one slow rotation. Yet high in its orange sky, powerful howling jet streams circle the moon in just one day at $270\,\text{miles per hour}$ ($120\,\text{m/s}$)—a phenomenon known as \textbf{Atmospheric Superrotation}.

\section*{Level 2: For the First-Year Undergrad — Clausius-Clapeyron Phase Equilibrium \& Hydrostatics}

\subsection*{1. Methane Vapor Pressure Equilibrium}
Near Titan's surface ($T_0 \approx 94\,\text{K}$), methane undergoes liquid-gas phase equilibrium governed by the Clausius-Clapeyron relation:
\begin{equation}
\ln\left(\frac{P_{\text{sat}}}{P_{\text{ref}}}\right) = -\frac{\Delta H_{\text{vap}}}{R_g} \left( \frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{\text{ref}}} \right)
\end{equation}
With $\Delta H_{\text{vap}} \approx 8.18\,\text{kJ/mol}$ and $T_{\text{ref}} = 111.6\,\text{K}$ ($1\,\text{bar}$), the saturation vapor pressure at $94\,\text{K}$ is $P_{\text{sat}} \approx 0.16\,\text{bar}$, accounting for the observed $5 - 8\%$ surface methane humidity.

\subsection*{2. Thermal Wind Equation in a Slowly Rotating Atmosphere}
In a cyclostrophically balanced superrotating atmosphere where centrifugal force balances the meridional pressure gradient:
\begin{equation}
\frac{u^2 \tan\phi}{R_T} + 2 \Omega u \sin\phi = -\frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = -\frac{R_{\text{spec}} T}{P} \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to log-pressure $z^* \equiv -\ln(P/P_0)$ gives the thermal wind relation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial (u^2)}{\partial z^*} = -\frac{R_{\text{spec}}}{\tan\phi} \frac{\partial T}{\partial \phi}
\end{equation}
Observing a pole-to-equator temperature gradient $\Delta T \approx 15 - 20\,\text{K}$ in the stratosphere directly predicts equatorial jet speeds $u_{\text{jet}} \approx 120\,\text{m/s}$.

\subsection*{1. Hide's Theorem \& Up-Gradient Eddy Momentum Transport}
Maintaining equatorial superrotation ($u_{\text{eq}} > \Omega R_T$) requires an up-gradient flux of angular momentum driven by planetary-scale wave breaking:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \bar{M}}{\partial t} = -\frac{1}{\cos\phi} \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \overline{u' v'} \cos^2\phi \right) - \frac{1}{\rho_0} \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \rho_0 \overline{u' w'} \cos\phi \right) + F_{\text{drag}}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{u' v'} < 0$ in the Northern Hemisphere advects angular momentum equatorward from high latitudes.
