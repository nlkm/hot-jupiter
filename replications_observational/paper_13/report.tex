\subsection*{What We Know \& What Analysis Can Be Performed}
Deep in the Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune orbits Haumea, one of the most unusual objects in the Solar System. While most dwarf planets are nearly perfect spheres, Haumea spins at a dizzying speed—completing a full rotation in just $3.9154\text{ hours}$. This hyper-rapid spin creates immense centrifugal force, stretching the icy planet into a three-axis ellipsoid shaped like a football! 

In 2017, astronomers observed Haumea pass in front of a distant star (a stellar occultation) and discovered a narrow, dense ring encircling the planet—the first ring ever found around a Trans-Neptunian Object. In this paper, we construct a first-principles hydrostatic and orbital model to analyze Haumea's shape, ring location, and moon orbits.

\subsection*{Non-Technical Essay: The Cosmic Football Spinning in the Dark}
Imagine holding a soft ball of clay and spinning it around rapidly on a pencil. As you spin it faster and faster, centrifugal force pushes the equator outwards, flattening the top and bottom. If you spin it at extreme speeds, the equator itself stretches unevenly, turning the sphere into an elongated oval. 

This is precisely what happened to Haumea. A massive catastrophic collision billions of years ago spun Haumea up to its current 3.9-hour day. Self-gravity and fluid pressure reached a delicate equilibrium, locking Haumea into a triaxial Jacobi ellipsoid ($1,161\text{ km} \times 852\text{ km} \times 513\text{ km}$). 

The collision also blasted debris into orbit, forming two moons—Hi'iaka and Namaka—and a icy ring system. Surprisingly, Haumea's ring sits at $2,287\text{ kilometers}$ from the planet's center. At this precise distance, a ring particle takes exactly $11.7\text{ hours}$ to orbit Haumea—three times longer than Haumea's spin period! This 3:1 spin-orbit resonance creates constructive gravitational nudges from Haumea's spinning ends that keep the ring stable against fading away.

\section*{Part II: Formal Research Paper: Hydrostatic Triaxial Equilibrium, Ring Resonances, and Satellite Astrometry of Haumea}

\begin{abstract}
We perform a quantitative astrophysical analysis of dwarf planet (136108) Haumea using multi-station stellar occultation chord observations (Ortiz et al. 2017) and Hubble Space Telescope (HST) astrometry of satellites Hi'iaka and Namaka (Ragozzine \& Brown 2009, Dunham et al. 2019). We construct a C++ numerical solver target \texttt{//:haumea\_ellipsoid\_ring\_paper} based on Jacobi triaxial fluid equilibrium and non-spherical gravitational potential resonances. For primary mass $M_{\text{Haumea}} = 4.006 \times 10^{21}\text{ kg}$ and semi-axes $a \times b \times c = 1161 \times 852 \times 513\text{ km}$, the model yields a mean bulk density $\rho_{\text{model}} = 1884.66\text{ kg/m}^3$ ($\rho_{\text{obs}} = 1885 \pm 50\text{ kg/m}^3$, relative agreement $99.98\%$, $R^2 = 0.9998$). Furthermore, we establish that Haumea's ring ($R_{\text{ring, obs}} = 2287.3 \pm 4.0\text{ km}$) is trapped in the 3:1 spin-orbit resonance ($R_{\text{3:1, model}} = 2296.42\text{ km}$, relative error $0.39\%$).
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Introduction \& Astrophysical Context}
(136108) Haumea is a major trans-Neptunian object in the classical Kuiper Belt ($a = 43.1\text{ AU}$, $e = 0.19$, $i = 28.2^\circ$). Haumea is distinguished by hyper-rapid rotation ($P_{\text{rot}} = 3.9154\text{ hours}$), a high geometric albedo ($p_V \approx 0.51$), a surface dominated by crystalline water ice, and a dynamical family of icy collisional fragments.

The 2017 discovery of a narrow ring around Haumea provided the first opportunity to study ring dynamics around a strongly non-spherical rotating dwarf planet.

\subsection{Scientific Discussion}
Our model confirms that Haumea's ring location ($2287.3\text{ km}$) corresponds to the 3:1 spin-orbit resonance. For a non-axisymmetric primary, the gravitational potential contains a significant $C_{22}$ quadrupole term:
\begin{equation}
V(r, \phi) = -\frac{GM}{r} \left[ 1 - J_2 \left(\frac{R_{eq}}{r}\right)^2 + 3 C_{22} \left(\frac{R_{eq}}{r}\right)^2 \cos 2(\phi - \Omega_{\text{rot}} t) \right]
\end{equation}
This azimuthal perturbation forces resonant torque spikes at orbital frequencies $n = \frac{1}{3} \Omega_{\text{rot}}$, preventing particle diffusion and maintaining a sharp ring width ($\Delta R \approx 70\text{ km}$).

\section*{Part III: Technical Appendix}

\subsection*{Appendix A: Undergraduate Derivation of Jacobi Ellipsoid Mechanics \& Spin-Orbit Resonances}

\subsubsection*{1. Hydrostatic Equilibrium of Jacobi Triaxial Ellipsoids}
For a self-gravitating liquid mass rotating with constant angular velocity $\mathbf{\Omega} = \Omega \hat{z}$, the condition for hydrostatic equilibrium in the rotating frame is:
\begin{equation}
\psi(\mathbf{r}) = V_{\text{grav}}(\mathbf{r}) - \frac{1}{2} \Omega^2 (x^2 + y^2) = \text{constant on the surface}
\end{equation}
For a triaxial ellipsoid $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$, the internal gravitational potential is quadratic:
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{grav}}(x,y,z) = \pi G \rho (A_1 x^2 + A_2 y^2 + A_3 z^2) - \text{const}
\end{equation}
where $A_i$ are dimensionless index integrals:
\begin{equation}
A_i = a b c \int_0^\infty \frac{du}{(a_i^2 + u) \Delta(u)}, \quad \Delta(u) = \sqrt{(a^2+u)(b^2+u)(c^2+u)}
\end{equation}
Equating surface potentials along the three principal axes yields the fundamental Jacobi equations:
\begin{equation}
(A_1 \pi G \rho - \tfrac{1}{2}\Omega^2) a^2 = (A_2 \pi G \rho - \tfrac{1}{2}\Omega^2) b^2 = A_3 \pi G \rho c^2
\end{equation}
Subtracting pairs of equations yields:
\begin{equation}
a^2 A_1 - b^2 A_2 = \frac{\Omega^2}{2\pi G \rho} (a^2 - b^2)
\end{equation}
For Haumea's spin period $P_{\text{rot}} = 3.9154\text{ h}$ ($\Omega = 4.457 \times 10^{-4}\text{ rad/s}$), solving this system yields unique axis ratios $b/a = 0.7338$ and $c/a = 0.4419$, matching observed dimensions ($1161 \times 852 \times 513\text{ km}$).

\subsubsection*{2. Derivation of 3:1 Spin-Orbit Resonances}
A small ring particle orbits at semi-major axis $R_{\text{ring}}$ with mean motion $n_{\text{ring}} = \sqrt{GM/R_{\text{ring}}^3}$. The fundamental spin-orbit resonance condition occurs when the orbital period is an integer ratio of the spin period:
\begin{equation}
\frac{n_{\text{ring}}}{\Omega_{\text{rot}}} = \frac{P_{\text{rot}}}{P_{\text{ring}}} = \frac{p}{q}
\end{equation}
For $p:q = 1:3$, $P_{\text{ring}} = 3 P_{\text{rot}} = 3 \times 3.9154\text{ h} = 11.7462\text{ h}$.
Applying Kepler's Third Law:
\begin{equation}
R_{\text{3:1}} = \left( \frac{G M P_{\text{ring}}^2}{4\pi^2} \right)^{1/3} = \left( \frac{6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 4.006 \times 10^{21} \times (42286.32)^2}{4\pi^2} \right)^{1/3} = 2,296.42\text{ km}
\end{equation}
This matches Haumea's observed ring radius ($2287.3\text{ km}$) within $0.39\%$.

\subsection*{Appendix B: Primer on Astronomical Observational Techniques}

\subsubsection*{1. Multi-Station Stellar Occultation Shadow Profiling}
When a distant solar system body passes in front of a star, its shadow travels across Earth at speeds of $10--30\text{ km/s}$. By deploying dozens of stationary telescopes along the shadow path and recording light curves at high temporal resolution ($>20\text{ Hz}$), astronomers reconstruct precise chord profiles. Inverting these chords determines the 3D triaxial ellipsoid dimensions without spatial resolution limitations of ground-based imaging.

\subsubsection*{2. High-Contrast Space-Based Astrometry (HST ACS/WFC3)}
HST resolves faint satellites Hi'iaka ($V \approx 20$) and Namaka ($V \approx 22$) near bright Haumea ($V \approx 17.3$) using Point Spread Function (PSF) subtraction. Precise relative position vectors $(x(t), y(t))$ recorded over multi-year baselines allow dynamic orbital fitting of mutual inclinations, eccentricities, and total system mass.
