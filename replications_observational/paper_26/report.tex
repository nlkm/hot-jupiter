\section*{Level 1: For the Non-Scientist — Tasting the Air of an Alien World}

\subsection*{The Big Picture}
Imagine being able to "taste" the atmosphere of a giant planet orbiting a distant star 700 light-years away, without ever leaving Earth. That is exactly what NASA's James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) achieved with \textbf{WASP-39b}—a warm, puffy gas giant roughly the mass of Saturn but inflated to larger than Jupiter. 

\subsection*{How Do We See an Atmosphere Across Trillions of Miles?}
When WASP-39b crosses directly in front of its home star (an event called a \textit{transit}), most of the starlight is blocked by the solid body of the planet. However, a tiny sliver of starlight passes through the outer ring of the planet's transparent atmosphere. Different gases in the atmosphere absorb very specific colors (wavelengths) of infrared light:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Water vapor ($\mathrm{H_2O}$) absorbs light at $1.4$ and $2.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, making the planet look slightly bigger at those colors.
    \item Carbon dioxide ($\mathrm{CO_2}$) creates a massive absorption spike at $4.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.
    \item Sulfur dioxide ($\mathrm{SO_2}$) creates an unmistakable chemical fingerprint at $4.05\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{The Exciting Discovery: Alien Smog and Photochemistry}
The detection of sulfur dioxide ($\mathrm{SO_2}$) on WASP-39b was the first time scientists ever witnessed \textit{photochemistry} in action on an exoplanet. Just like sunlight triggers chemical reactions in Earth's atmosphere to create the ozone layer (or city smog), ultraviolet rays from WASP-39's star break apart water molecules, freeing up oxygen and hydrogen to react with hydrogen sulfide ($\mathrm{H_2S}$) and forge sulfur dioxide high in the alien sky.

\section*{Level 2: For the First-Year Undergrad — Atmospheric Scale Heights \& Radiative Cross-Sections}

\subsection*{1. Derivation of the Atmospheric Scale Height}
In an isothermal atmosphere in hydrostatic equilibrium, the balance between the upward pressure gradient and downward gravity is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho(z) g
\end{equation}
Applying the ideal gas law $P = \rho k_B T / \mu m_H$ (where $\mu$ is the mean molecular weight and $m_H$ is the mass of a hydrogen atom):
\begin{equation}
\frac{dP}{dz} = -\frac{\mu m_H g}{k_B T} P \implies P(z) = P_0 \exp\left(-\frac{z}{H}\right), \quad H \equiv \frac{k_B T}{\mu m_H g}
\end{equation}
For WASP-39b with $T_{\text{eq}} \approx 1166\,\text{K}$, $g = G M_p / R_p^2 \approx 4.14\,\text{m/s}^2$, and a hydrogen-dominated envelope ($\mu \approx 2.3$), the scale height is exceptionally large:
\begin{equation}
H = \frac{(1.38 \times 10^{-23}\,\text{J/K})(1166\,\text{K})}{(2.3)(1.67 \times 10^{-27}\,\text{kg})(4.14\,\text{m/s}^2)} \approx 1.01 \times 10^6\,\text{m} = 1010\,\text{km}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{2. Wavelength-Dependent Transit Depth}
The effective transit radius $R_p(\lambda)$ of a planet at wavelength $\lambda$ corresponds to the radial impact parameter $b$ where the slant optical depth $\tau(\lambda, b) \approx 0.56$:
\begin{equation}
R_p(\lambda) \approx R_0 + H \ln\left( \frac{\kappa(\lambda) P_0}{\mu g} \sqrt{\frac{2\pi R_p}{H}} \right)
\end{equation}
Therefore, each scale height of absorption enhances the observed transit depth $\delta(\lambda) \equiv (R_p(\lambda)/R_\star)^2$ by:
\begin{equation}
\Delta \delta \approx \frac{2 R_p H}{R_\star^2} \approx 2 \left(\frac{1.28 R_J}{0.93 R_\odot}\right) \left(\frac{820\,\text{km}}{0.93 R_\odot}\right) \approx 240\,\text{ppm per scale height}
\end{equation}
This large amplitude makes WASP-39b one of the most prominent transmission spectroscopy targets in the galaxy.

\subsection*{1. Forward Model Architecture}
Our atmospheric transmission code calculates the slant chord transmission through 100 plane-parallel layers:
\begin{equation}
\tau(\lambda, b) = 2 \int_b^\infty \sum_i n_i(r) \sigma_i(\lambda, T(r), P(r)) \frac{r dr}{\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_i(\lambda)$ are molecular absorption cross-sections sampled at $R = 10,000$ resolution from the ExoMol and HITRAN databases, and $n_i(r)$ are chemical volume mixing ratios computed under chemical and photochemical equilibrium.

\subsection*{2. Photochemical $\mathrm{SO_2}$ Kinetic Network}
The production of $\mathrm{SO_2}$ in warm irradiated atmospheres proceeds via NUV photolysis of water vapor:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{H_2O} + h\nu (\lambda < 200\,\text{nm}) \longrightarrow \mathrm{OH} + \mathrm{H}
\end{equation}
followed by radical oxidation of hydrogen sulfide:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{H_2S} + \mathrm{H} \longrightarrow \mathrm{HS} + \mathrm{H_2}, \quad \mathrm{HS} + \mathrm{O} \longrightarrow \mathrm{SO} + \mathrm{H}, \quad \mathrm{SO} + \mathrm{OH} \longrightarrow \mathrm{SO_2} + \mathrm{H}
\end{equation}
Matching the observed $4.05\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ transit depth ($21,420\,\text{ppm}$) requires an $\mathrm{SO_2}$ mixing ratio $\chi_{\mathrm{SO_2}} \approx (1.0 \pm 0.3) \times 10^{-5}$ and a super-solar atmospheric metallicity $[M/H] \approx +1.0$ ($10\times$ Solar).
