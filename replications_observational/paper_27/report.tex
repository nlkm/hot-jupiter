\section*{Level 1: For the Non-Scientist — An Alien Ocean Beneath the Ice}

\subsection*{The Big Picture}
Beneath the cracked, icy surface of Jupiter's moon \textbf{Europa} lies a global saltwater ocean containing more liquid water than all of Earth's oceans combined. Even though Europa orbits in the deep freeze of outer space—where surface temperatures hover around a frigid $-170^\circ\text{C}$ ($100\,\text{K}$)—its ocean remains liquid. How is this possible?

\subsection*{The Cosmic Dough-Kneader: Tidal Flexing}
Europa does not circle Jupiter in a perfect circle; its orbit is slightly non-circular (eccentric), maintained by a gravitational gravitational tango with neighboring moons Io and Ganymede (the \textit{Laplace resonance}).
\begin{itemize}
    \item When Europa is closer to Jupiter, the giant planet's immense gravity stretches the moon into an egg shape.
    \item When Europa moves further away, the gravitational pull weakens, and the moon snaps back toward a sphere.
\end{itemize}
This continuous stretching and flexing creates friction deep within the ice and rocky mantle—just like bending a paperclip back and forth until the metal becomes hot to the touch. This tidal friction pumps roughly $800\,\text{billion Watts}$ ($0.78\,\text{TW}$) of heat into Europa's interior, melting the underside of the ice shell and maintaining a warm, liquid ocean.

\section*{Level 2: For the First-Year Undergrad — Steady-State Heat Conduction \& Resonant Flexing}

\subsection*{1. 1D Steady-State Heat Conduction through an Ice Shell}
In thermal equilibrium, heat transported outward through an ice shell of thickness $D_{\text{ice}}$ by conductive diffusion is governed by Fourier's Law of heat conduction:
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{cond}} = -k \frac{dT}{dz} = k \frac{T_{\text{melt}} - T_{\text{surf}}}{D_{\text{ice}}}
\end{equation}
where $k \approx 3.0\,\text{W/(m\,K)}$ is the thermal conductivity of water ice, $T_{\text{melt}} \approx 270\,\text{K}$ is the basal ocean melting temperature, and $T_{\text{surf}} \approx 100\,\text{K}$ is the radiative surface temperature.
For an observed equilibrium heat flux $F_{\text{eq}} \approx 25.5\,\text{mW/m}^2$:
\begin{equation}
D_{\text{ice}} = \frac{k (T_{\text{melt}} - T_{\text{surf}})}{F_{\text{cond}}} = \frac{(3.0\,\text{W/m\,K})(170\,\text{K})}{0.0255\,\text{W/m}^2} \approx 2.00 \times 10^4\,\text{m} = 20.0\,\text{km}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{2. Viscoelastic Tidal Dissipation Power}
Under orbital eccentricity $e$, the diurnal tidal flexing generates internal volumetric dissipation power $P_{\text{tide}}$:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{tide}} = \frac{21}{2} \left(\frac{k_2}{Q}\right) \frac{G M_{\text{Jup}}^2 R_{\text{Eur}}^5 n e^2}{a^6}
\end{equation}
For Europa ($M_{\text{Jup}} = 1.898 \times 10^{27}\,\text{kg}$, $R_{\text{Eur}} = 1560\,\text{km}$, $a = 670,900\,\text{km}$, $e = 0.009$, $n = 2.05 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{rad/s}$), matching $P_{\text{tide}} \approx 7.8 \times 10^{11}\,\text{W}$ yields an effective viscoelastic tidal response $k_2 / Q \approx 0.0025$.

\subsection*{1. Maxwell Viscoelastic Shell Rheology}
The volumetric tidal dissipation rate $\dot{\epsilon}_{\text{tide}}$ in an ice shell with complex shear modulus $\mu^* = \mu_0 / (1 + i / (\omega \tau_M))$ is:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\epsilon}_{\text{tide}}(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{2} \text{Im}[\mu^*] |\boldsymbol{\sigma} : \boldsymbol{\epsilon}| = \frac{\mu_0 \omega \tau_M}{1 + \omega^2 \tau_M^2} |\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(r, \theta)|^2
\end{equation}
where $\tau_M \equiv \eta(T) / \mu_0$ is the Maxwell relaxation time. Because ice viscosity decreases exponentially with temperature $\eta(T) = \eta_0 \exp[E^* / (R_g T)]$, tidal dissipation is heavily concentrated in the warm, ductile lower ice shell ($T > 240\,\text{K}$).

\subsection*{2. Electromagnetic Induction in a Saline Subsurface Ocean}
Galileo magnetometer (MAG) passes E4, E14, and E26 detected an induced magnetic dipole moment $B_{\text{ind}} \approx 220\,\text{nT}$ in response to Jupiter's rotating magnetosphere ($B_0 \approx 400\,\text{nT}$ at $\omega_{\text{syn}} = 1.76 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{rad/s}$). The induced magnetic response function for a conducting shell of conductivity $\sigma$ and depth $D_{\text{ocean}}$ is:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{ind}} = -B_0 \left( \frac{R_{\text{ocean}}}{r} \right)^3 \left[ 1 - \frac{3}{\alpha R_{\text{ocean}}} \coth(\alpha R_{\text{ocean}}) + \frac{3}{\alpha^2 R_{\text{ocean}}^2} \right] \boldsymbol{m}_{\text{dip}}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \equiv \sqrt{i \mu_0 \sigma \omega_{\text{syn}}}$. Inversion requires an ocean conductivity $\sigma \ge 2.5\,\text{S/m}$ (consistent with a terrestrial seawater composition, $[\mathrm{NaCl}] \approx 30\,\text{g/kg}$) and a liquid depth $D_{\text{ocean}} \ge 80\,\text{km}$.
